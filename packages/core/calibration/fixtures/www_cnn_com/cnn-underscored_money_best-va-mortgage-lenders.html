With no down payment or mortgage insurance requirements, low interest rates and flexible credit qualifications, VA loans provide an affordable path to homeownership for military borrowers. Backed by the US Department of Veterans Affairs, VA loans are offered by private lenders, including banks, credit unions and online mortgage companies.

VA mortgages have some of the lowest interest rates out there, with average rates sometimes as much as 0.5 percentage points lower than conventional mortgage rates. But to qualify, you must have a qualifying military service record, meet lenders’ credit requirements (often scores of 620 or better) and have a low amount of debt compared to your income.

We’ve narrowed the best VA mortgage lenders to our top eight picks, but if you need a VA loan refresher course before diving in, familiarize yourself with the types of VA loans.

What is a VA loan?

A VA loan is a mortgage guaranteed by the US Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and is available to service members, veterans and eligible family members. Created as part of the Servicemen’s Readjustment Act of 1944 (also known as the G.I. Bill), the VA home loan program was initiated to help military members become homeowners.

Since its inception, the VA home loan program has helped over 28 million military members purchase, build, refinance or repair their homes. Today, over 3.7 million veterans have VA home loans.

While all mortgage rates are elevated compared to recent years, VA loan interest rates are nearly always lower than conventional (non-government) mortgages and often beat FHA loan and USDA loan rates.

Do I qualify for a VA loan? Requirements and eligibility

To qualify for a VA loan, your military service must meet eligibility guidelines. Additionally, individual lenders set their own VA loan requirements for credit and income.

Military service

You must meet the VA’s minimum service requirements based on your status and when you served.

﻿Type of borrowerService requirements
Service members
A minimum of 90 continuous days.
Veterans, National Guard and Reserve members
Requirements vary by service date but are commonly between 90 days and 24 months. You must have served the full period you were called to active duty.
Spouses
Surviving spouses of veterans who died from a service-related disability, are missing in action or are prisoners of war
Additional eligible borrowers
US citizens who served in the Armed Forces of a government allied with the United States in World War II and members of specific organizations (including Public Health Service officers and cadets at the US Military, Air Force or Coast Guard Academies).

If you don’t meet the minimum service requirements, you may still qualify for the VA home loan benefit if you were discharged for hardship, a qualified medical condition, service-related disability or other eligible reason.

VA home loan Certificate of Eligibility

A Certificate of Eligibility (COE) is a document that shows your lender you’ve met the VA’s minimum service requirements and qualify for the home loan benefit. You must provide it to your lender before applying for a loan. You can request a copy of your COE online or by mail via the VA, or your lender may be able to help you with the request.

You don’t need your COE to begin the mortgage application process, but you must provide it to your lender before closing.

Credit requirements

The VA home loan program doesn’t set a minimum credit score, but many lenders look for credit scores of 620 or higher. Some VA lenders accept scores as low as 500.

Down payment and funding fee requirements

One of the most attractive features of VA loans is that they don’t require a down payment, which means you can finance 100% of the home purchase. Conventional mortgages typically require a down payment of at least 3% of the purchase price.

However, a down payment may be necessary in some instances, such as when purchasing a home for more than its appraised value or buying a home above conforming loan limits ($766,550 in most areas) while you already have a VA loan.

You may also consider saving for a down payment to lower your loan amount and monthly payment. A down payment of at least 5% or more will reduce your VA funding fee, a required upfront charge that helps support the VA program.

But what about mortgage insurance?

With a VA loan, you won’t pay for mortgage insurance, regardless of whether you make a down payment. In contrast, conventional mortgages require mortgage insurance if you don’t put down at least 20%.

Types of VA loans

The VA home loan program includes several loan options for purchasing, building, refinancing or repairing a home. For most loan options, you’ll work with an approved VA lender.

  • Purchase loan: With a VA-backed purchase loan, you can buy, build or repair your home with no down payment requirement. VA purchase loans have below-market interest rates and can be used on various property types, including approved condos and single-family homes up to four units.
  • Interest rate reduction refinance loan (IRRRL): If you’re a current VA borrower, you can use this streamlined refinance option to reduce your interest rate, change your mortgage term length or adjust your interest rate structure. VA refinance rates for IRRRLs are typically higher than purchase loans.
  • Cash-out refinance: With a VA cash-out refinance, you can refinance your existing VA loan and tap your home’s equity for cash. You can also use this loan type to refinance a non-VA mortgage into a VA loan. Interest rates are typically higher than purchase loans and IRRRLs.
  • Native American Direct Loan (NADL): If you’re a Native American veteran or a veteran married to a Native American, the NADL program can help you purchase, build, repair or refinance your home at lower interest rates than other VA mortgages. With this loan option, you’ll borrow directly from the VA rather than an approved lender.
  • Energy-efficient mortgage: This is a loan you can borrow in addition to a purchase loan or IRRRL, and it allows you to make energy-efficient home improvements up to $6,000.
  • Manufactured home loan: VA purchase loans can be used on a manufactured home or lot. Some lenders prohibit borrowers from using funds for this purpose, so check with your preferred lender before applying.
  • Adapted housing grants: The VA offers housing grants to eligible disabled veterans and service members purchasing a home or making accessible modifications to an existing property.

VA loans can be an affordable loan option for eligible homebuyers. The competitive interest rates, no down payment and flexible credit qualifications help make homeownership accessible if you have limited savings and less-than-perfect credit. Closing costs are also typically lower — the VA prohibits lenders from charging more than 1% of the loan amount in origination fees, which is less than most conventional lenders charge.

However, the upfront funding fee adds additional costs to your loan. And if you choose to roll the funding fee into your mortgage, you could owe more than your home is worth. Also, some lenders may impose a minimum credit score or additional requirements.

6 steps to get a VA loan

1. Obtain a Certificate of Eligibility (COE)

The first step to getting a VA loan is to request your COE, which shows your lender you’ve met the VA’s service requirements and qualify for the home loan benefit. You can request a COE online, via mail or through a VA-approved lender. While you don’t need to have your COE before starting the application process, it’s a good idea to confirm your eligibility ahead of time to avoid delays.

2. Review your credit and finances

Before contacting a lender, you’ll want to understand your financial situation. Using a budget will help you manage your current expenses and be realistic about how much you can afford for a mortgage payment.

Start by checking your credit scores to know where you stand. Higher credit scores make you more likely to qualify for a VA loan and receive lower interest rates. Your current financial institution or credit card issuer may provide free access to your scores, or you can buy them from a third-party service.

You’ll also want to request and review your free credit report from each of the three major credit bureaus via AnnualCreditReport.com and dispute any inaccurate information. Make all debt payments on time before and during the mortgage process to improve your credit scores.

How to get a VA loan with bad credit

Buying a house with bad credit may be possible, but you’ll have fewer lender options and can expect to pay higher interest rates. If your scores are low, you’re more likely to be approved if you have compensating factors, such as a large down payment or low debt-to-income ratio.

Many lenders require a minimum score of 620, but some may accept scores as low as 500. When searching for a lender, ask about their borrower requirements to find one with flexible credit qualifications.

3. Compare VA mortgage lenders

You’ll apply through an approved VA lender for most loan types. (To get an NADL, apply directly with the VA.) While your current bank or credit union may offer VA loans, contacting multiple lenders is essential as interest rates, fees and requirements vary. According to Freddie Mac, you can save between $600 and $1,200 per year by getting rate quotes from two to four lenders.

During your research, compare the loans’ APRs (which reflect the annual cost of borrowing, including loan fees) to see the true cost of each loan. Here’s a look at how a few $300,000 VA loans with 30-year terms stack up:

Focusing on interest rates alone can be misleading. At first glance, the loan at 5.99% seems to offer the best deal because it has the lowest interest rate, but comparing the APRs reveals that the loan at 6.00% provides the lowest cost because it has fewer included fees.

So, while getting a competitive interest rate is crucial, it’s not the only consideration when choosing a loan. Some VA mortgage lenders’ rates may only be available if your debt-to-income ratio is under a certain threshold or you purchase discount points. Whether lender fees are assessed as a set amount or a percentage of your loan principal can also impact your costs.

When comparing VA mortgage lenders, ask about fees not included in the APR, borrower requirements and any loan features you’re looking for.

4. Submit an application

In addition to your COE, you’ll need to provide your lender with financial documents and other personal information.

When you apply for a mortgage, lenders typically request the following:

  • Pay stubs for the past 30 to 60 days
  • W-2s, 1099s and tax returns for the past two years
  • Two most recent bank statements
  • Documentation of additional income sources
  • Government-issued form of ID
  • Social Security number

5. Find a home and get a VA appraisal

While you work with a lender to arrange financing, you’ll also choose a real estate agent and begin looking at homes. It’s helpful to work with an agent who is familiar with VA loans, as they’ll also understand the property requirements.

Once you select a home, you and your agent will submit the purchase agreement to your lender, who will order an appraisal to confirm the home’s value and ensure it meets the VA’s minimum property requirements. While a home inspection isn’t required, the VA recommends that all buyers get one. An inspection can reveal problems (big and small) with the home that may impact your offer or interest in purchasing it.

6. Underwriting and closing

Your lender will begin processing your home loan while you’re home shopping and finalize it once you select a property. During this process, called mortgage underwriting, your lender may require additional information regarding your finances or the property. It’s important to respond to these requests promptly to avoid delays.

Three business days before your loan closes, your lender will provide a Closing Disclosure that contains your final loan details and the amount of cash required at closing. Review this document carefully and ask any questions before your closing date. Your lender will also let you know whether you need to wire the closing costs or bring a certified check to closing.

Methodology

Our editors combined forces with our data research team to collect more than 450 data points to determine the best VA mortgage lenders. Ultimately, we rated the 28 lenders under consideration across the following four categories:

Loan cost (30%)

The cost of a loan is critical, especially for mortgages that include decades-long repayment. Here, we rewarded lenders for prominently displaying interest rates, offering rate discounts and allowing you to lock your rate.

Loan details (30%)

Here, we drilled down to the basics of each lender’s products, looking at repayment term options, average closing timelines and types of VA loans offered. We also awarded bonus points to lenders that close a high volume of VA loans each year, since they are more likely to be familiar with program requirements.

Eligibility (20%)

This category was simple: Can most military borrowers and veterans qualify for a mortgage with this lender? We weighed criteria like geographic availability, minimum credit score requirements and whether the lender considers alternative credit data.

Customer experience (20%)

After you close your home loan, you want a mortgage lender or servicer that helps you along — and doesn’t get in your way — during repayment. Lenders that fared best here have helpful websites, physical branch locations and few major blemishes across independent organizations like the Better Business Bureau (BBB), Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) and the Nationwide Mortgage Licensing System (NMLS).

What didn’t make the cut

Twenty of the VA home loan providers we reviewed didn’t rank highly enough to be included in our list of the best VA mortgage lenders. The most common flaw among lenders that didn’t make the cut was a lack of transparency around interest rates and eligibility requirements.

  • United Wholesale Mortgage closes a high volume of VA loans each year but doesn’t publicly disclose its interest rates or many loan details.
  • LoanDepot can offer loan preapproval in as little as 15 minutes but charges high interest rates and fees.
  • Freedom Mortgage accepts borrowers with bad credit but has a poor track record of borrower satisfaction and a high number of complaints with the CFPB.
  • Fairway Independent Mortgage received high marks on J.D. Power’s consumer satisfaction survey but doesn’t disclose its VA loan rates or the time it takes to close a VA loan.

Frequently asked questions (FAQs)

VA loans don’t have a down payment requirement, but you can choose to make a down payment to lower your mortgage amount and monthly payment. Some situations may require a down payment, such as if the purchase price exceeds the home’s appraised value.

VA loan rates are typically lower than conventional rates. However, your rate depends on several factors, including lender, credit profile, location, loan amount, term length and interest rate structure.

You may not be eligible for VA benefits if you were dishonorably discharged; however, you can try to qualify by applying for a discharge upgrade or correction.

VA borrowers pay a one-time funding fee ranging from 1.25% to 3.3% of the loan principal, depending on the VA loan type and whether it’s your first VA mortgage. You may pay the fee upfront or finance it with your loan (though the latter option can result in owing more than the home is worth). You’ll also pay standard closing costs, including an appraisal fee, title insurance and loan origination. VA loans have lower closing costs than other mortgage types, as lenders can only charge up to 1% of the loan amount for their origination fee.

There is no limit to how many times you can use your VA home loan benefit as long as you remain eligible. If you’re taking out a VA loan and still own a home with a current VA loan, the new mortgage may be subject to loan limits, or you may have to make a down payment.
