Background

Creation of the District of Columbia

The Residence Act of 1790, enacted by Congress on July 16, authorized President George Washington to select a location for a federal district no more than ten miles square along the Potomac River, serving as the permanent seat of the U.S. government.[5] This legislation aimed to establish a neutral territory independent of any state, fulfilling Article I, Section 8 of the Constitution, which granted Congress exclusive jurisdiction over such a district. In anticipation of federal needs, Virginia had already passed an act on December 3, 1789, ceding to the United States a ten-mile square territory that included the town of Alexandria and surrounding lands from Fairfax County, offering both sides of the river for the district to promote national unity and strategic positioning. Maryland followed with its own cession in 1791, completing the territorial contributions. President Washington appointed three commissioners to oversee the project, selected the site in January 1791 and engaged French engineer Pierre Charles L'Enfant to design the federal city, incorporating Alexandria specifically for its established port facilities, deep-water access on the Potomac, and to equitably balance the land contributions from both states. L'Enfant's plan envisioned a grand capital spanning the river, with Alexandria serving as a key commercial gateway. The inclusion of Alexandria was driven by its strategic river access, which was expected to facilitate trade and position it as a vital economic hub within the federal district, complementing the new city's development.[6] Virginia's cession was formally accepted, but full transfer of jurisdiction over the Virginia portion, including Alexandria, occurred via an organic act by Congress in early 1801, marking the district's operational establishment. Initial expectations were optimistic, with Alexandria's residents and leaders anticipating growth in commerce and population under federal oversight, leveraging its proximity to the capital for enhanced prosperity.

Economic Challenges in Alexandria

Following its incorporation into the District of Columbia in 1801, Alexandria experienced significant disruptions to its trade networks with Virginia, as federal authorities treated commerce within the District as internal rather than interstate, imposing restrictions that severed direct economic ties to the state. Previously a bustling port facilitating the flow of goods like tobacco, flour, and whiskey to Virginia markets, Alexandria's merchants faced barriers to exporting produce and importing supplies without navigating cumbersome federal oversight, which prioritized the District's overall interests over local needs. This shift contributed to a stagnation in commercial activity, as Virginia traders sought alternative routes to avoid the regulatory hurdles.[7] The anticipated economic boost from the Potomac Canal, completed in 1830 to connect the upper Potomac River to tidewater, largely failed to materialize for Alexandria. While the canal aimed to enhance navigation and trade volume through the port, it instead intensified competition from nearby Georgetown, which benefited from better integration with federal infrastructure projects, and from the rival port of Baltimore, which drew away much of the regional shipping traffic via superior rail connections. As a result, Alexandria's wharves saw diminished usage relative to competitors, with canal traffic doing little to offset the port's declining share of Potomac Valley commerce.[8] Compounding these issues, Congress's decision in 1802 to abolish Alexandria's independent municipal government led to profound administrative inefficiencies, as local decision-making on taxation, infrastructure maintenance, and market regulations fell under distant federal control from Washington. Without autonomous governance, the city struggled to address immediate needs like road repairs and fire protection, resulting in delayed responses to economic downturns and a perception of neglect that eroded business confidence. U.S. Census data reflects mixed outcomes: Alexandria's population grew from 4,971 in 1800 to 8,678 by 1840, but per capita economic indicators suggested challenges amid regional shifts.[9] Alexandria's economy also grappled with tensions arising from its reliance on slave labor, particularly in the domestic slave trade that became a cornerstone of the port after 1820, yet federal oversight in the District curtailed local autonomy in managing this sector. Enslaved individuals powered much of the warehousing, shipping, and artisan work, but restrictions on local ordinances—such as those governing slave auctions and hiring practices—created uncertainties that deterred investment and complicated operations under national jurisdiction. This friction highlighted the mismatch between Alexandria's Virginia-rooted economic model and the District's federal framework, further hampering growth and contributing to calls for retrocession, culminating in a 1846 referendum and congressional approval on July 9, 1846.[10]

Path to Retrocession

Early Proposals and Debates

In the early 1820s, residents of Alexandria began organizing formal efforts to seek retrocession from the District of Columbia back to Virginia, motivated by growing frustrations over their lack of political representation and economic isolation from the burgeoning capital city across the Potomac River. Efforts to lobby for retrocession marked one of the first structured local advocacy groups dedicated to the cause. These efforts highlighted key grievances, including the principle of taxation without representation, as Alexandria's citizens were subject to federal taxes to support District infrastructure but held no voting rights in Congress and could not elect representatives to influence national policy. Throughout the 1820s and into the 1830s, these arguments gained traction through petitions submitted to both Congress and the Virginia General Assembly, emphasizing additional jurisdictional conflicts such as the inability of Alexandria's militia to operate effectively under federal control and the town's exclusion from state-level benefits like internal improvements funding. A notable petition in 1822 led to the introduction of a bill in the U.S. House of Representatives by Virginia congressman Alexander Smyth, which proposed retroceding the county of Alexandria to Virginia but was ultimately rejected amid concerns over altering the federal district's boundaries. Similar legislative attempts followed, including a 1835 bill that also failed to pass, as federal officials expressed fears that approving retrocession would set a dangerous precedent for other portions of the District or even challenge the constitutional integrity of the seat of government. Local leaders played a pivotal role in sustaining momentum during this period, with figures like Dr. Phineas Janney, a prominent Alexandria physician and community figure, emerging as a key organizer who rallied support through public town meetings and correspondence campaigns in the 1840s. Janney's advocacy, often framed in letters to newspapers and lawmakers, underscored the practical hardships faced by residents, such as diminished trade opportunities compared to Georgetown and Washington, and helped mobilize broader community backing for the petitions. These early debates and failed initiatives laid the groundwork for later successes, though they faced consistent resistance from those prioritizing the District's unified federal status.

Congressional Legislation

The retrocession of Alexandria County from the District of Columbia to Virginia was enabled by federal legislation passed during the 29th United States Congress. In early 1846, Representative George W. Hopkins of Virginia introduced a bill proposing the return of the county to Virginia jurisdiction. The bill gained momentum amid ongoing economic grievances in Alexandria and passed the House of Representatives on July 5, 1846, after committee review by the House Committee on the District of Columbia.[11] Key provisions of the act, approved as Chapter 35 of the Statutes at Large (9 Stat. 35) on July 9, 1846, stipulated that the retrocession would occur only upon acceptance by the Virginia General Assembly and a favorable majority vote among the white male inhabitants of Alexandria County and the town of Alexandria in a referendum.[11] The legislation explicitly excluded all federal properties, buildings, and fortifications within the county from the transfer, ensuring continued national control over key installations such as the Marine Barracks and the Washington Navy Yard's approaches. It also preserved existing laws and debts of the District applicable to the ceded territory until Virginia's formal acceptance.[11] In the Senate, debates centered on the constitutional implications of altering the federal district established by the Residence Act of 1790, as well as concerns over the expansion of slavery's influence, given Alexandria's role in the interstate slave trade. Senators like John Davis of Massachusetts argued that retrocession set a dangerous precedent for diminishing the seat of government, while southern members emphasized states' rights and local economic relief. Despite these contentions, the Senate passed the bill without amendments on July 9, 1846. President James K. Polk signed the act into law on July 9, 1846, shortly after the declaration of the Mexican-American War on May 13, 1846, which overshadowed but did not derail the proceedings. The Virginia General Assembly responded swiftly, ratifying the act on July 26, 1846, via joint resolution without modifications, thereby paving the way for the subsequent referendum held on September 2, 1846, which approved retrocession by a vote of 794 to 162 among white male voters, effective September 9, 1846.[12] This legislative sequence marked the culmination of years of advocacy for retrocession, focusing on restoring Virginia's sovereignty over its former ceded lands.[12]

The 1846 Referendum

Campaign Efforts

In the summer of 1846, following congressional approval of retrocession legislation, pro-retrocession committees formed across Alexandria County to galvanize local support for the upcoming referendum. These groups, spearheaded by influential residents including former U.S. Congressman Charles Simms, coordinated public rallies and meetings to build enthusiasm among voters frustrated with federal oversight. Campaign slogans such as "Home Rule" captured the push for local self-governance, while calls for restoring Virginia's judicial protections highlighted concerns over federal interference in local affairs, including commerce and property rights. Proponents distributed pamphlets that underscored the potential economic revival under Virginia's trade laws, arguing that reintegration would eliminate congressional restrictions on banking and interstate commerce that had stifled Alexandria's port activities since 1801. Organized opposition remained limited, largely confined to federal employees who favored maintaining the status quo; their efforts consisted of subdued anti-retrocession meetings that failed to generate widespread counter-mobilization. To ensure informed participation, campaigners educated voters on the referendum's binding nature under the congressional act, emphasizing that a majority approval would irrevocably transfer the county back to Virginia.

Voting Process and Results

The referendum on the retrocession of Alexandria County from the District of Columbia to Virginia was conducted over two days, on September 1 and 2, 1846, at various precincts throughout the county. Polling locations were established in the town of Alexandria and surrounding rural areas, with the process supervised by three commissioners appointed by President James K. Polk as stipulated in the enabling legislation passed by Congress on July 9, 1846. These commissioners ensured compliance with Virginia's election laws, including the requirement for voters to take an oath affirming their residency and eligibility. Eligibility followed Virginia's election laws for white male citizens over 21 who met property requirements, a group estimated at approximately 1,200 individuals based on contemporary census and tax records. Turnout reached about 80%, with 985 total ballots cast, reflecting strong community engagement following months of public debate. The results showed 763 votes in favor of retrocession and 222 opposed, a decisive margin that met the act's requirement for a popular majority. Voting patterns varied by area: in the town of Alexandria, support was overwhelming with 734 yes and 116 no (about 86%), while rural precincts strongly opposed it with 29 yes and 106 no (about 21%).[13] Immediate reactions in Alexandria were jubilant, culminating in celebratory parades on September 3, 1846, where residents marched through the streets accompanied by music, bonfires, and public toasts to Virginia's reabsorption of the territory. Local newspapers described the events as a "grand illumination" with fireworks and gatherings at key landmarks like Market Square. The commissioners promptly certified the results as valid and free of irregularities, forwarding the official tally to President James K. Polk on September 4, 1846. Polk issued a proclamation on September 7 confirming the vote's outcome and authorizing the transfer effective September 9.[13]

Implementation and Aftermath

Transfer of Governance

Following President James K. Polk's proclamation on September 7, 1846, announcing the favorable referendum outcome, the retrocession of Alexandria County was pending Virginia's formal legislative acceptance.[13] The process was governed by the federal Retrocession Act of July 9, 1846, which conditioned the transfer on the referendum and Virginia's acceptance. Virginia's General Assembly accepted the retrocession on February 27, 1847, after which the transfer took effect, marking the start of the administrative transition back to Virginia control. Virginia assumed full civil jurisdiction over the county effective February 27, 1847, with federal authority ceasing on that date as per the act and Virginia's resolutions. This timeline allowed for a structured shift, avoiding prolonged uncertainty in public administration. Federal commissioners oversaw the inventory and physical transfer of county records, the local jail on St. Asaph Street, and key infrastructure such as roads and public buildings from District of Columbia officials to their Virginia counterparts, completing the bulk of these handovers in early 1847.[8] These assets, previously under federal oversight, were documented meticulously to preserve legal continuity. In parallel, Virginia's governor appointed interim magistrates and a sheriff for Alexandria County to replace the outgoing D.C. levy court members, with the appointments taking effect upon the transfer. The courts of the District of Columbia in the retroceded area were formally dissolved, redirecting judicial authority to Virginia's state system. Ongoing legal cases from the federal period were transferred to Virginia courts for resolution, while the federal government absolved the county of outstanding local taxes owed to the District, relieving residents of accumulated debts as part of the retrocession terms.[14] The transition saw minor disruptions, including brief confusion over the validity of District-issued currency in local trade and adjustments to interstate commerce regulations along the Potomac River, which were resolved within weeks through bilateral federal-Virginia agreements.[8] Overall, the process was orderly, reflecting months of preparatory negotiations between the two governments.

Long-Term Consequences

Following the retrocession of Alexandria to Virginia in 1846, the city's economy experienced a notable recovery, particularly in its port activities, as it now benefited from state-level infrastructure investments and taxation policies unavailable under federal oversight. Previously hampered by congressional inaction on improvements, Alexandria's harbor saw revitalization through Virginia's authorization of dredging and wharf enhancements, leading to increased trade volume by 1850 compared to pre-retrocession levels. This resurgence positioned Alexandria as a key node in the Chesapeake trade network, with exports of flour, tobacco, and lumber driving local commerce. The reintegration into Virginia also reinforced the institution of slavery in the region, aligning Alexandria with the state's pro-slavery legal framework that permitted expanded local practices, including the operation of slave pens and auctions without the federal restrictions that had begun to emerge in the District. This shift allowed unchecked enforcement of slave codes until the 1860s, contributing to Alexandria serving as a major hub in the domestic slave trade, with thousands of enslaved individuals trafficked through its markets annually in the decade following retrocession. Demographically, Alexandria's population grew from approximately 7,500 in 1846 to around 12,000 by 1860, fueled by economic opportunities from deeper integration into Virginia's agricultural and mercantile systems, attracting immigrants and free Black workers alongside enslaved labor. This growth reflected broader prosperity but also underscored persistent racial hierarchies under state governance. Nationally, the retrocession sparked ongoing debates about precedents for territorial adjustments, influencing discussions on District of Columbia governance reforms—such as proposals for limited home rule—but ultimately discouraging further retrocessions due to concerns over federal authority erosion. It did not lead to similar actions elsewhere, though it highlighted tensions between state sovereignty and national control. The event exacerbated sectional tensions leading into the Civil War, as Northern critics viewed it as a concession to Southern slaveholding interests, amplifying disputes over federal power and the balance between free and slave states in the capital region. This underscored the growing North-South divide, with Alexandria's return symbolizing Virginia's resistance to antislavery influences in Washington.
