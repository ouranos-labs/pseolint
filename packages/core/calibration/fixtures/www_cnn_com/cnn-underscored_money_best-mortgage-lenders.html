10 best mortgage lenders of March 2024 — and how to compare them

By

Erin Gobler

Andrew Pentis
Katie Lowery,

CNN Underscored Money

Updated 2:18 PM EDT, Tue January 23, 2024

Given the high cost of homebuying, a mortgage is necessary for the vast majority of aspiring homeowners. And finding the right lender can make the difference of thousands of dollars — or even tens of thousands — in loan costs.

There are many mortgage lenders to choose from, which makes it possible for most borrowers to find a match. However, the sheer volume of options can also be overwhelming.

To aid your search, we’ve rounded up the 10 best mortgage lenders. Each of these lenders is best suited to certain borrowers and situations. By reading each lender’s features, pros and cons, you’ll increase your odds of finding the right fit for you.

To determine the lenders with the best mortgage rates and overall terms, our editors cast a wide net, surveying more than 40 financial institutions. We compared them across 27 criteria, including interest rate options, eligibility requirements, closing speed, and more. Why get a mortgage?

  • Access to homeownership: The key benefit of having a mortgage is that it creates a path to homeownership. Most people can’t afford to pay for a home in cash, so a mortgage helps you buy one and pay it off over an extended period.
  • Long repayment terms: Mortgages have long repayment terms, with the most common being 30 years. These long repayment terms help keep your monthly payments more affordable (at the cost of more overall interest). However, you can also choose a shorter loan term if it fits your needs.
  • Many loan types: You can find one that works for you — whether a conventional or a government-backed loan, a fixed mortgage or an adjustable rate — based on your credit scores, income, veteran status and other factors.
  • Fixed housing costs: Most mortgages have fixed interest rates, meaning your monthly payments remain stable for your entire loan term. Contrast that with the cost of renting, which often increases each year.
  • Government support: The federal government has several loan programs that help you buy a home if you don’t have the credit history, income or savings to qualify for a conventional mortgage. These loans often have easier eligibility requirements and favorable loan terms (though they may also come with fees or mandatory mortgage insurance premiums).

How does a mortgage work?

A mortgage is a contractual agreement between a borrower and a lender. The lender agrees to give the borrower the money they need to buy a home. In return, the borrower agrees to make monthly payments until the loan is repaid. And if the borrower fails to make their payments, the mortgage gives the lender the right to foreclose on the home.

Mortgages have a few important features, including the principal amount, interest rate and repayment term. The loan is amortized over the entire loan term, and the percentage of each payment that goes toward your principal increases each month.

Example: Suppose you buy a home with a 30-year loan with a beginning balance of $250,000 at an interest rate of 6.534%. Your monthly payment would be $1,586, accounting for only principal and interest.

In the first month, $1,361 of your payment goes toward interest, while the other $225 goes toward your principal balance. It’s not until the 19th year of your mortgage that the money going toward principal each month exceeds the money going toward interest. After 30 years, your loan balance will be fully repaid.

Types of mortgages

First, a home loan is either a fixed-rate or an adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM). A fixed-rate loan has the same interest rate for the entire loan term, as well as the same monthly payment. An ARM, on the other hand, has a fluctuating interest rate. After an initial fixed-rate period, your mortgage rate and monthly payment can rise and fall with market rates.

Besides rate types, here are the most popular loan types, most of which can be either fixed-rate mortgages or ARMs:

  • Conventional loan: Any loan that isn’t backed by a government program. Most conventional loans are conforming, but they can also be jumbo loans.
  • Conforming loan: A loan that meets the maximum loan limits set by the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) for eligibility to be purchased by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac (which lessens the lender’s risk and, in turn, may allow them to lend to less creditworthy applicants or offer lower interest rates). In 2024, the conforming loan limit in most areas of the country is $766,550 and $1,149,825 in designated high-cost areas, according to the FHFA.
  • FHA loan: A loan by a private lender that’s backed by the Federal Housing Administration to help borrowers (perhaps with low credit scores or little savings) access homeownership.
  • VA loan: A loan by a private lender that’s backed by the Department of Veterans Affairs to help veterans and service members buy homes with no down payment or lower credit scores.
  • USDA loan: A loan by a private lender that’s backed by the Department of Agriculture to help low and moderate-income borrowers buy homes in qualifying rural areas.
  • Jumbo loan: A loan that exceeds the maximum loan limits for conforming loans, often with stricter eligibility requirements due to increased risk to the lender.

How to compare mortgage rates

Your mortgage interest rate will significantly impact your overall loan costs, so it’s important to shop around and compare rates before getting a loan.

First, you can compare current mortgage rates and APRs by simply viewing them on a lender’s website. This step can give you a good idea of which lenders may offer the most competitive rates. However, these rates aren’t personalized to you, so it’s important to take it a step further.

Tip: Compare APRs (or annual percentage rates) instead of simple interest rates since they account for both the base rate and any lender fees. Put another way: APRs give you a more complete picture of a loan’s cost and help you make apples-to-apples comparisons between lenders.

The next step in comparing mortgage rates is to get preapproved with a handful of lenders. In most cases, this will impact your credit scores. However, multiple mortgage preapprovals in a short period will only count as a single inquiry on your credit.

Once you’ve gotten preapproved, you’ll get more detailed information about the interest rate you qualify for, as well as any discount points or fees that may increase or decrease your APR. Once you’ve received estimates from several lenders, you’ll have a better idea of which can offer you the best rate.

9 steps to getting a mortgage

  1. Review your finances. Check your credit reports (via AnnualCreditReport.com) and credit scores (via your bank, credit card issuer or a third party), and calculate your debt-to-income ratio to get an idea of what type of mortgage you might be eligible for.
  2. Know your budget. When you apply for a loan, your mortgage lender will preapprove you for a certain amount, but that amount might be more than you’re comfortable spending. It’s best to know what monthly payment best fits into your budget upfront. A mortgage payment calculator can help.
  3. Consider your loan options. There are many home loan types, including conventional and government-backed loans. Explore your options to see which you’re eligible for and which is the best fit for your situation.
  4. Save your down payment. Most loans require a down payment, and conventional loans require at least 3% down — or more, depending on your financial situation. A 20% down payment can help you avoid mortgage insurance and lessen your monthly dues. Start saving early.
  5. Shop around for lenders. There are many lenders to choose from, and there isn’t one best lender for everyone. Explore your options to see which lenders are the best fit for the mortgage you need. If you’ll be a new homeowner, you might prioritize lenders with expansive first-time homebuyer programs.
  6. Get preapproved. Before you make an offer on a home, it’s best to get preapproved for your loan. Consider getting preapproved by multiple lenders to ensure you have several interest rates to compare and choose from. Not only can this help you find the best rate, but having a preapproval letter in hand can show sellers that you’re a serious buyer.
  7. Complete your application. Once you’ve made an offer on a home and it’s been accepted, you’ll submit a formal application to your preferred lender (ideally one that offers a rate lock). This step requires you to share in-depth and accurate information about your finances.
  8. Go through underwriting. The underwriting process is when your lender takes a deep dive into your finances to determine whether you qualify for a loan. During this process, you may be required to provide additional information about your finances.
  9. Close on your loan. Finally, once underwriting is complete and you’ve gone through your property’s inspection and appraisal, you can close on your home loan. At the closing, you’ll pay your down payment and closing costs and sign your loan documents.

Methodology

After exhaustive research and thorough analysis, 10 of the 42 financial institutions under consideration cracked the list of our best mortgage lenders. Their out-of-5 star ratings were informed by our objective rubric, which included the following four categories:

Rates (35%)

We considered seven criteria in the cost-oriented category, including how competitive and transparent lenders’ rates are and whether they offer rate locks and float-downs.

Loan details (35%)

Here, we drilled down to the basics of each lender’s products, looking at their various mortgage types and programs. We also evaluated:

  • Down payment requirements
  • Preapproval timelines
  • Closing speeds
  • Fees

Eligibility (10%)

Lenders that scored well in this category offer mortgages nationwide and pair relatively low minimum credit score requirements with other accessible underwriting criteria.

Repayment experience (20%)

In addition to J.D. Power studies referenced above, our editorial team also relied on independent sources like the Better Business Bureau and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. We put each lender’s customer service, mobile app, marketing materials and track record under the microscope.

What didn’t make the cut

Thirty-two banks, credit unions and mortgage companies aren’t among the 10 lenders with best mortgage rates, according to our analysis. Those that didn’t register a sufficient out-of-5 star rating were deficient in multiple criteria we considered. Here are a few examples of where some notable lenders fell short:

  • Rocket Mortgage could offer more information about its programs before asking shoppers for their information.
  • Better Mortgage could offer more types of mortgages, beyond conventional and FHA.
  • Flagstar Bank could more prominently share its rates with shoppers.
Frequently asked questions (FAQs)

Credit scores, income, debt-to-income ratio and down payment size are among the most important factors. However, the criteria lenders consider vary by mortgage type.

Certain lenders may have different profit goals or overhead expenses that require that they charge more. Additionally, some lenders may have a higher or lower appetite for risk.

Your mortgage rate is the interest rate set by your lender that represents the cost of borrowing money. Your APR — short for annual percentage rate — shows all of your loan costs, including your interest rate and other fees.

A mortgage lender is a financial institution that lends money — a mortgage broker doesn’t. Instead, it works as a middle-person by helping borrowers find the best lenders for their situation.
